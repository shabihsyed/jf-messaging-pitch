JF Messaging Manifesto · Updated for the December 1 launch

More code isn’t more value.

Almost every engineering team now runs AI coding agents. Code throughput has roughly doubled, agents are opening and merging pull requests on their own, and AI spend is the fastest-growing line in the R&D budget. Fewer than half of engineering leaders can say what any of it is worth. This manifesto is the story Jellyfish tells to close that gap, in the order a first call should tell it, and the words that stay the same across every page, deck and campaign launching December 1.

The shift

We built the category that measures engineering. AI agents made it obsolete.

Every engineering metric the industry built assumes a person on the other end: a commit has an author, a PR has a reviewer, headcount is the cost model. In 2026 all three came loose. Output came loose from people, as agents started opening PRs with no engineer driving. Cost came loose from value, as token spend per merged PR climbed 60x in a year. And the quality gate disappeared, as more PRs merged with AI as the only reviewer.

Ask four people in the same org and you get four answers. The engineer says they’re shipping 3x faster. The manager says reviews are piling up and they can’t tell what the agent did. The VP asks whether the spend is paying off. The CTO asks whether quality is holding. Same tools, four expectations, no shared number.

What Jellyfish is

Jellyfish 2.0: the Agentic Engineering Intelligence platform.

We call it the Jelly AI platform. For engineering teams running an AI-native SDLC who can’t prove what their agents return, the Jelly AI platform puts people, AI-assisted work and autonomous agents in one data model. It ties that work to delivery, quality and cost, and benchmarks it against 1,000+ engineering organizations. Unlike metrics built for human-only teams, or vendor dashboards that only count their own usage, it measures human + agent work side by side, in the metrics leadership already trusts.

The Jelly AI platform is built in four layers, and every one of them is a reason to buy instead of build.

Announcement Blog

We Built the Category That Measures Engineering Productivity. AI Agents Made It Obsolete.

So we tore it down and rebuilt Jellyfish for the AI-native SDLC.

For almost a decade, Jellyfish has helped engineering leaders answer one question: what is my team actually doing, and is it working? More than 1,000 companies run on that answer. We helped define the category that produces it, software engineering intelligence.

This year, AI agents broke it.

Not slowly, and not at the edges. The metrics our industry built for human-only teams stopped telling the truth around the time agents started opening pull requests. Dashboards stayed green while the work underneath them changed shape. Leaders kept reporting throughput that no longer meant what it used to mean.

We could have bolted an AI tab onto the product and called it a strategy. Instead we did the harder thing. We rebuilt Jellyfish from the data platform up, around a different unit of work and a different set of questions. This post is about why.

Engineering intelligence was built to count people

Every engineering metric you know assumes a person on the other end. A commit has an author. A ticket has an assignee. A pull request is opened by an engineer, reviewed by another engineer, and merged when a human says it is ready. Cycle time, throughput, DORA, allocation: all of it rolls up from human activity, because for the entire history of software, human activity was the work.

That assumption made the category possible. If you could see what people did, you could see what the organization produced, what it cost, and where it got stuck. The pull request was the atomic unit. Code review was the quality gate. Headcount was the cost model.

It was a good model. It is no longer the right one.

Three things broke at once

We have an unusual view of this. Jellyfish Research tracks how 1,300+ companies, 294,000 engineers and 110 million pull requests are building software right now. Here is what changed in 2026.

1. Output came loose from people. At the top 1% of companies, 9.4% of merged pull requests are now opened by a software factory agent with no engineer driving the tool at all. Six months ago that number was 1.6%. When a growing share of the work is opened by something that isn’t on the org chart, any metric that divides output by headcount is quietly measuring something else.

2. Cost came loose from value. Token spend per merged pull request rose 60x in twelve months. If that money were buying proportionally more product, it would be the best investment in the building. It isn’t. What ships on the other side has grown nowhere near 60x, and almost no one can say which part of that spend is paying off and which is simply burning.

3. The quality gate disappeared. At the top 10% of companies, 8.5% of pull requests now merge with AI as the only reviewer. Code review was the checkpoint every level of the organization relied on: where engineers got feedback, where managers saw quality, and where delivery metrics came from. When one in twelve changes skips a human entirely, the PR still shows what changed. It no longer shows how or why.

Put those together and you get the situation most CTOs describe to us. Engineers say they are shipping three times faster. Managers say reviews are piling up and they can’t tell what the agent did. The VP asks whether the spend is paying off. The CTO asks whether quality is holding. Same tools, four answers, no shared number.

The three questions every board now asks

In our State of Engineering Management research, most organizations said they are seeing real gains from AI. Fewer than half said they can measure those gains in any real way. The rest are running on gut feel, and the faster their process changes, the harder gut feel is to defend.

When we talk to CTOs, their boards, their CFOs and their investors, the conversation keeps landing on the same three questions:

  1. Where do we stand? How much of our work is done by people, by people using AI, and by agents on their own, and how does that compare to everyone else?
  2. Are we transforming? Is all this new code turning into shipped value, or are we just moving the bottleneck to review and CI and calling it velocity?
  3. What is it worth? What are we spending on AI, what are we spending it on, and what are we getting for it?

A tool built to count human commits cannot answer any of them. Vendor dashboards cannot either: they show usage of their own product and never connect it to throughput or business value. So leaders stitch it together by hand, tab-hopping across vendor portals, Git logs and spreadsheets the week before the board meeting.

That is the gap. It is the reason we rebuilt.

What we rebuilt: Agentic Engineering Intelligence

We stopped thinking of Jellyfish as software engineering intelligence and started building Agentic Engineering Intelligence: a platform where people, AI-assisted work and autonomous agents are first-class citizens in the same data model, measured with the same metrics, tied to the same outcomes. Under the hood that meant a new data platform, with faster analytics, more scalable storage, and the lineage to take in new signals as fast as the tools change. On top of it, the product is now organized around the three questions.

Where do we stand?
Any metric leadership already trusts, like PRs merged or cycle time, broken down by human contributors and autonomous agents. Custom metrics built from a plain-English request, the way your org defines productivity. Benchmarks against 1,000+ engineering organizations. And an assistant you can ask why a number moved, which comes back with the likely cause instead of a chart to dig through.
Are we transforming?
Leading indicators from how engineers actually work with their agents: which skills they use, how interactive their sessions are, which practices are spreading on their own. Cohorts that compare heavy and light adopters of a practice against rework and cycle time, so you can tell what to scale in weeks instead of waiting for word of mouth. A lifecycle view you can reshape to your own AI-native workflow, so you can see exactly where the bottleneck moved.
What is it worth?
AI spend across every vendor, model and team in one view, next to total R&D cost. Spend mapped to the work it went into, using the same investment categories you already use for people. Cost per merged pull request, split between human and AI, tracked over time. The unit economics finance has been asking for.

Customers are already using it this way. LastPass rolled Claude out to 100% of 200 engineers, set a pre-AI baseline in Jellyfish, and watched the issue lifecycle shorten by 30 to 40%, with its best teams delivering two to three times faster. Loadsmart saw AI generate 86% of its code and review swell to a quarter of delivery time, then paired AI coding with AI review and cut that delay to under 2% while feature velocity doubled.

The next unit of work is the session

Here is the bet we are making about where this goes.

The pull request was the atomic unit of engineering because it was the moment a human handed work to the team. In an agentic world, that moment happens earlier. Every piece of AI-written code starts as a session: a person states an intent, an agent does the work, and a PR comes out the other end. The PR shows you the result. The session shows you how it got there and why: which skills the agent used or skipped, how clear the ask was, what context it had, what it spent, and whether it proved its work before anyone was asked to review it.

By the time a pull request is opened, quality has already been decided. It was decided in the session. And today, that session lives and dies on an engineer’s laptop and on the machine running an autonomous agent (like Devin).

That is why we are building Agent Observatory: every session, from every agent your teams use, captured from intent to outcome, linked to its commits and its PR, scored against what the person set out to do, and confirmed later by whether the code held up. Coaching for the author and a heads-up for the reviewer land right on the PR. Prompts are analyzed, never shown, and scores describe the session and the setup, never rank the person. Early Access opens December 1.

We think whoever understands the session will own the next decade of engineering intelligence, the same way whoever understood the pull request owned the last one.

What this means if you run engineering, or fund it

How We Built ShipMate and Used Jellyfish to Measure Its Impact

Every engineering team is building its own AI skills right now. We did too. Building it got us far. Proving it worked took a platform.

By Nathan Klingensmith, Jellyfish Engineering

If you run an engineering team in 2026, you probably have a ShipMate of your own. Maybe it is a folder of Claude skills someone started on a Friday, or a prompt playbook that lives in a pinned Slack message. Ours started at our company kickoff, grew into a companion that takes a ticket all the way to a merged PR, and now runs about 26 times a week for the average engineer who uses it.

This is the story of how we built it, what we got wrong about adoption, and how we used Jellyfish to answer the question every team building internal AI tooling eventually gets asked: is this actually making us faster?

It started with a talk about a “super skill”

At our company kickoff, a guest speaker showed a persona-based “super skill” their team used. It wasn’t a pile of one-off skills where you have to remember which one to call. It was a persona that could look at what was going on, knew everything it could pull on, and acted on it.

I had already experimented with skills and hadn’t found them very useful. This was different. LLMs are really interpreters, so what if you gave one all the knowledge it needed to interpret the work and just do it? I started coding the first version that day, at kickoff.

It was originally called Shipman, a play on the punny names other teams gave their super skills. Too many people read it as a person’s last name, and it was more gendered than it needed to be, so it became ShipMate. I still love the name.

What ShipMate actually does

ShipMate is a companion for our software development lifecycle. It holds everything that is specific to how Jellyfish builds software. The easiest way to picture it: if we hired a new engineer tomorrow, what would we tell them about how we work? Those are the things we teach ShipMate instead.

So now I can say “ShipMate, do this ticket,” and it takes it from there:

  1. Reads the ticket, sees it isn’t assigned, and assigns it to you
  2. Implements the change and writes the tests
  3. Drafts the pull request, in plain language
  4. Responds to the agentic review comments, then to the humans
  5. Fixes CI until it’s green

None of this was new work. Engineers were already doing every one of these steps, either by hand or by telling Claude to do them one at a time. ShipMate boils that knowledge down into one playbook.

Start with the most annoying job

The very first ShipMate skill resolved Cursor Bugbot comments. We had just turned on Bugbot, everyone was dealing with it, and reading agentic review comments and replying to each one did not feel like a good use of a human’s time. It was a problem Claude could obviously handle.

That skill became the building block. Once we could resolve bot comments, we added a skill to draft the PR, then one to resolve human comments, then rolled them into a playbook for getting a PR all the way through. ShipMate grew one painful step at a time.

If we hired a new Jellyfish tomorrow, what would we tell them about how we do software? Those are the things we teach ShipMate instead.

Nathan Klingensmith

Adoption was slow, and that was expected

Every engineer does software development a little differently, with the tools and environment they like. I knew getting people to change would be hard, and I knew I had to build ShipMate around how Jellyfish thinks about code, not how I do. About half of our engineers use it today.

Two things moved people. The first was demos that showed ShipMate going end to end at the same quality they would produce themselves. The second was control. Some engineers were wary of handing Claude that much, so the first time you run ShipMate it asks how involved you want to be: fully hands-off, stop whenever there is a concern, or just write the code and let you review it. Nobody has to go hands-free on day one.

The moment it clicked

Earlier this year we moved from Jira to Linear and added a triage queue, where new bug tickets land in a team’s queue until someone picks them up. It added real noise. Suddenly there were three bug tickets and no time to look at them. With ShipMate, I could spin up a new Claude session, say “do this ticket,” and come back twenty minutes later to a PR ready for review.

The other moment is smaller. One of ShipMate’s skills writes the PR description, which is a slow job for a human and something LLMs are very good at. I still get a little joy every time I see a PR written in the ShipMate style, because I know someone didn’t have to sit there and write all those words.

Build the most adaptable thing, not the most powerful

There were plenty of things I could have hard-coded: which model to use, whether to spin up git worktrees, whether to run extra local servers to test changes in parallel. I deliberately held those back. Changing the model changes the quality of the output. Worktrees mess with someone’s file system. Extra servers eat their ports. Some people would hate all of that.

So ShipMate encodes the most generic piece of value a skill can deliver, and leaves environment choices to the engineer. The same philosophy carries into Fleet, the system I’m building now to manage many ShipMate sessions at once: primitive building blocks, with anything opinionated as an optional add-on.

We also built it in the open. We post plans and direction in our AI development channel and treat it as the feedback loop. Early adopters get to shape what ShipMate becomes, and we try to apply feedback fast enough that people feel heard.

Proving it worked, with Jellyfish

After a few months, and ten weeks with three of us working on it instead of one, we had to answer the real question: is ShipMate making a measurable difference? Feedback in a channel tells you what people like. It doesn’t tell you whether they ship more.

We built a ShipMate analytics dashboard in Jellyfish using three of the newest things in the platform. Without them, this analysis wouldn’t have been possible at all:

Custom metrics
To count ShipMate invocations specifically, alongside the delivery metrics we already trusted.
Cohorts
To group engineers by how much they use ShipMate: top users, active users and idle users.
Dashboards
To roll it all up in one place and share it with the rest of engineering and leadership.

Invocations per person showed a clear second inflection around August 16, so we used that as our before-and-after line. Then we compared each cohort against itself: idle users before and after, active users before and after, top users before and after. That kept us from the easy trap of concluding that the best engineers use ShipMate, so of course ShipMate users are faster. We were asking whether the same people got faster.

The story held up. The more someone used ShipMate, the bigger their gains in issues resolved and pull requests merged after adoption. Idle users moved the least. Our top users run it on nearly every ticket; I use it on every single one. The dataset is still small, because ShipMate hasn’t been around long, but the direction is clear.

We didn’t conflate “the best engineers use ShipMate, so they’re faster.” We asked whether the same people got faster after they started using it.

Nathan Klingensmith

If you’re building your own ShipMate

  1. Start with the job nobody wants. One painful, universal step makes the value obvious and gets the first people in.
  2. Encode how your company builds, not how you do. Think about what you would teach a new hire.
  3. Let people choose how hands-off to be. Control drives adoption more than features do.
  4. Keep it adaptable. Ship the generic building blocks and make the opinionated parts optional.
  5. Measure the same people before and after. DIY tooling gets you a long way. Proving it works takes skill-level data joined to delivery data, which is what Jellyfish gave us.

Building ShipMate ourselves was the right call. Measuring it ourselves would not have been. If your team is building its own skills and agents and needs to show what they return, we’ll show you how we did it, in your own data.
